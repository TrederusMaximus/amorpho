# 49 — Combat Pillars and Biological Roster Strategy, v0

**Status:** qualitative combat-design-space pass using owner-supplied design direction. It supplies the counter-pressure [48](48_ROSTER_TIMING_AND_ONE_CONSCIOUSNESS_BIOLOGICAL_OPPORTUNITY_TRACE_V0.md) found missing, and states what any future concrete combat system must satisfy. It defines no move, combo, input, control, frame data, health value, stamina, mana, guard or shield meter, damage formula, class, archetype, tier, species combat role, loadout, matchup value or engine, and it researches no species.

> **Biology says what a body costs to risk; combat says what it is worth risking for.**

## 1. Purpose

Doc 48 showed that biology alone already makes *which body?* a real decision — and that, left alone, it would push the Warden toward whichever body is biologically cheapest to interrupt. A satisfying fighting game needs the other side of the scale:

> What makes one Amorpho a better tactical choice than another for a particular encounter, when biological cost alone would favour the cheapest body?

A biologically expensive body may still be worth deploying because it is better suited to the situation, better protected, differently equipped, more familiar to the Warden, configured for a particular style, better positioned, or otherwise better able to achieve the objective. This pass names that design space. It sets no numbers and decides no mechanics.

## 2. Two separate axes

> **Biological Deployment Cost** — what the Warden risks or interrupts by embodying this individual now: lost Productive Opportunity, interrupted recovery, current manifestation risk, Core exposure risk, and the spatial and rooting consequence of moving the core ([45](45_REMAINING_PRODUCTIVE_OPPORTUNITY_QUALITATIVE_COMPOSITION_V0.md), [40](40_ROOTED_MATURE_LEAF_RECOVERY_CEILING_AND_COMPENSATORY_REMODELING_V0.md), [37](37_MANIFESTATION_DESTRUCTION_TUBER_CORE_VIABILITY_AND_PHYSICAL_DROP_V0.md), [48](48_ROSTER_TIMING_AND_ONE_CONSCIOUSNESS_BIOLOGICAL_OPPORTUNITY_TRACE_V0.md)).

> **Combat Suitability** — how appropriate the available embodied form, its current condition and its preparation are for the actual conflict and its objective.

They are **separate decision axes, and neither determines the other** (AMO-D161):

- the biologically cheapest body is not automatically the tactically best body;
- the tactically best body is not automatically biologically cheap to use.

That tension is the roster decision. No score combines the axes, and no system names a best fighter.

## 3. Combat does not come from taxonomy

The combat-independence rule stands and is sharpened (AMO-D006, AMO-D162, [04](04_TRANSFORMATION_AND_COMBAT.md) §4). Real biology determines **which living manifestation exists**, its condition, its lifecycle and its persistent risk. It does **not** supply a combat ranking. Rejected:

- larger real species = stronger fighter; rarer species = better fighter;
- Bloom = automatically stronger than Leaf;
- geographic origin = combat type; difficult cultivation = higher attack power;
- taxonomic archetypes — *giant species = tank, small species = rogue, rare species = boss, tropical species = mage, Bloom = glass cannon*.

Combat identity must be **deliberately designed**, never inferred from botanical labels. Authored, species-referencing design remains what AMO-D006 allows — real characteristics as inspiration for silhouette, animation and theme, never as specification — and whether fighters are produced as bespoke kits, shared systems with species identity, or otherwise stays AMO-Q027's.

**Physical embodiment may still matter.** Future geometry may legitimately affect reach, silhouette, locomotion, collision and presentation. That is allowed — *physical embodiment may matter without turning real-world taxonomy into a power ladder* — and nothing is designed here (AMO-Q027, AMO-Q109).

> **Combat identity is built through gameplay, not assigned by taxonomy.** Future differentiation should be able to arise from Warden skill, learned technique, selected abilities, equipment, armour, magical protection, defensive configuration, movement choices, tactical style, current manifestation condition and the situational objective. No system is chosen.

## 4. The eleven pillars

### Pillar 1 — Skill Expression
Combat primarily rewards mastery: timing, spacing, positioning, defensive reads, commitment, risk assessment, execution, adaptation and tactical disengagement (L15, AMO-D008). The aspiration is a fighting game's learnable depth, without its assumption that every exchange ends in a knockout and a reset. No inputs are specified.

**Skill protects biology as well as winning.** A skilled Warden should be better not only at defeating or outmanoeuvring an opponent but at **keeping hostile effects from reaching the living body**. Better defence, positioning and judgement can mean less persistent biological damage (AMO-D163). This is distinctly Amorpho.

### Pillar 2 — Protection Management
Protection is the existing layer between hostile effects and the body — shields, wards, armour, worn protection, blocking, parrying, evasion, positioning, defensive abilities (AMO-D122, [35](35_COMBAT_PROTECTION_MANIFESTATION_BODY_DAMAGE_AND_BIOLOGICAL_PERSISTENCE_V0.md)).

> **Protection is not extra combat health; it is the Warden's means of preserving the living body.**

Its failure changes what the fight means:

```
PROTECTION HEALTHY      → exchanges may stay relatively controlled
PROTECTION DEGRADED     → body-reaching risk rises
BODY ALREADY DAMAGED    → each further exchange may carry more biological consequence
```

That is escalation without a meter. No resource system is designed.

### Pillar 3 — Mobility and Positioning
Movement matters for attack opportunity, defence, body preservation, escape, pursuit and objective control — and for **where the persistent core ends up**.

> **Movement is tactical combat action and persistent-world relocation at the same time.**

The Tuber travels inside the embodied Amorpho (AMO-D129). Continued embodiment keeps moving the individual; exit roots it where it stands (AMO-D032). So **body selection is also a relocation decision**: a fight far from a good rooting site leaves the Warden to root there or stay embodied to travel back, and while they travel Productive Return and recovery stay paused and World Time continues (AMO-D136, AMO-D145). Combat geography can therefore add biological opportunity cost by itself. No teleport, automatic return or travel model exists. Movement, geometry and disengagement must also hold up against **several attackers** — congestion, enclosure, pursuit and escape ([55](55_MULTI_ATTACKER_ENCOUNTERS_ESCAPE_AND_ANTI_DOGPILE_PRINCIPLES_V0.md), AMO-D174–AMO-D176).

### Pillar 4 — Offensive Commitment
Attacking should commit: **offensive actions expose the attacker to counterplay and risk**, so that position, timing and judgement decide more than stat comparison. No attack mechanic is defined.

**Offensive success is not body destruction.** It may mean forcing surrender, controlling space, forcing retreat, preventing escape, achieving an objective, or wearing down protection until the opponent yields (AMO-D124, AMO-D125). Biological injury is never the sole measure.

### Pillar 5 — Defence and Preservation
Defence has two simultaneous aims: stay tactically effective, and preserve the living manifestation. A defensive choice can succeed with **no offensive advantage at all** — conceding position to avoid a body-reaching hit and keep a valuable Leaf intact is valid skill expression.

### Pillar 6 — Risk Escalation
Combat supports escalating biological risk as a model, not as hard states:

```
CONTROLLED EXCHANGE
  ↓ PROTECTION UNDER PRESSURE
  ↓ BODY-RISK PHASE
  ↓ REAL MANIFESTATION DAMAGE
  ↓ WARDEN DECIDES WHETHER TO CONTINUE
  ↓ possible Functional Collapse
  ↓ possible fight to manifestation destruction
```

Escalation stays **player-driven** where possible: surrender, conscious withdrawal, escape or continuing. The system does not push ordinary fights toward destruction (AMO-D127, L59).

### Pillar 7 — Objective-Driven Combat
There is no single victory condition. Conflicts may be about defending, reaching, holding, escorting, preventing, delaying or contesting, none of which requires destroying the opponent. **The objective determines why the fight matters; body destruction is only one possible extreme outcome.** The conflict's stake is what makes biological risk worth taking at all, and what withdrawing concedes ([54](54_CONFLICT_STAKES_AND_COST_OF_WITHDRAWAL_V0.md), AMO-D172, AMO-D173). No mission type is designed (AMO-Q028, AMO-Q081).

### Pillar 8 — Disengagement Mastery
Knowing when to stop, how to create disengagement, how to preserve the body and whether one more exchange is worth the biological risk is **part of combat, not a failure of it** (AMO-D125, AMO-D163).

> A highly skilled Amorpho player should be recognisable not only by what they can defeat, but by how well they bring valuable manifestations through dangerous encounters.

No score or ranking expresses this.

*Revised 2026-10-07:* Pillars 3 (Mobility and Positioning), 5 (Defence and Preservation) and 8 (Disengagement Mastery) now have a qualitative interaction owner in [58](58_MOVEMENT_DEFENCE_AND_PROTECTION_INTERACTION_V0.md): physical movement and Threat Geometry, Active Defence distinct from Protection, and Break Contact, Pursuit and Interception (AMO-D177, AMO-D178). No combat class or archetype follows.

### Pillar 9 — Preparation
Protection choices, equipment, ability configuration, environmental preparation and body selection may all matter before the first exchange. **Combat begins before the first attack, with the decision of which body and which protection to risk.** No inventory or loadout is designed.

**Preparation must not erase skill.** Better equipment changes options, risk and tactical possibility; it never produces deterministic victory (L15). Two otherwise similar manifestations may fight very differently because of protection, defensive configuration, learned technique or later loadout choices — a likely source of differentiation that owes nothing to species.

### Pillar 10 — Persistent Consequence
The fight does not disappear when it resolves: damaged protection, biological scars, a lowered Recovery Ceiling, lost Productive Opportunity, a changed location, manifestation loss or an exposed core may all remain (AMO-D120, AMO-D141, AMO-D130). Nothing resets.

A conventional round-based fighter restores both characters for the next match; Amorpho generally cannot assume that. The next encounter may involve the same scarred body, a rehabilitated body, another manifestation, another individual, or no available body at all. Fighting-game rounds remain the in-encounter structure (AMO-D008); persistence is what surrounds them.

### Pillar 11 — Warden Continuity Across Bodies
The persistent combat actor is partly the **Warden**. A Warden fights through different Amorphos over time, so knowledge, tactical habits and mastery persist when the body changes. How technique is owned — by the Warden, the individual, or both — is not decided (AMO-Q027, AMO-Q099).

**The body still matters.** Not all Amorphos are interchangeable puppets: the chosen manifestation matters through its actual biological condition, legitimately used physical form, possible equipment compatibility, damage history and current protection (AMO-Q025, AMO-Q109). *The manifestation is a fighter because the Warden inhabits it, not because its species came with a combat class* (L16).

**Same intent on every platform.** These pillars are combat truth on desktop, console, mobile and VR alike. Inputs and physical expression may differ; VR may deepen physical defence, positioning and gesture, but body damage, protection, escalation and resolution stay canonical (AMO-D041, AMO-D104, AMO-Q059, AMO-Q060). No controls are designed. **Human combat is not used here**; it remains AMO-Q130.

## 5. Roster-strategy integration

```
THREAT / OBJECTIVE
        ▼
AVAILABLE AMORPHOS
        ▼
for each candidate:
    BIOLOGICAL DEPLOYMENT COST
    COMBAT SUITABILITY
    CURRENT PROTECTION AND BODY CONDITION
    SPATIAL CONSEQUENCE
        ▼
WARDEN JUDGEMENT  ──  no formula, no recommended fighter
        ▼
ONE BODY CHOSEN (AMO-D028)
        ▼
COMBAT → RESOLUTION → PERSISTENT CONSEQUENCE
```

**Both axes move.** Suitability changes with damage, protection loss, equipment, recovery and position; deployment cost changes with lifecycle. The same individual can pass from *expensive and useful* to *cheap and useful*, or any other combination.

**Opportunity cost is not total risk.** A senescent individual whose phase has closed through Dormancy Commitment no longer interrupts productive work when embodied, so it may be biologically cheaper to deploy — while its body may be declining, its commitment remains, and embodiment merely pauses its retirement ([46](46_DORMANCY_COMMITMENT_BOUNDARY_V0.md), AMO-D157). A terminal, doomed body has no productive future and still **carries the persistent core**, so destroying it still exposes that core (AMO-D117, AMO-D130). *Cheap Productive Opportunity cost is not cheap Core risk.* Neither is claimed to be a good fighter.

**Bloom is already distinct through risk.** It signals a substantial core and carries different exposure; no combat role is assigned (AMO-D144, L63).

**Combat should create dilemmas, not solve them.** The system should present competing good reasons to choose different bodies rather than calculating one answer — and understanding biology, risk, timing, condition, matchup and objective can itself become player skill. No information interface is designed (AMO-Q044). In play, **Biological Deployment Cost is judged under partial information** — location and visible condition are known, opportunity, Core security and recovery potential are inferred — and no system recommends a body ([50](50_WARDEN_BIOLOGICAL_INFORMATION_AND_ROSTER_JUDGEMENT_MODEL_V0.md), AMO-D164).

**No universal best Amorpho** is the design target: different situations should reward different preparation, style, protection, movement and bodies. No balance rule is imposed here.

## 6. Worked contrasts

**A — cheap body, poor fit.** B has broad biological availability and is cheap to interrupt, but its current preparation suits the threat poorly. A is biologically expensive and better suited. The Warden must choose; no answer is supplied.

**B — expensive body, unnecessary.** A is tactically excellent and sits in a highly valuable productive window. B is adequate. Using A would sacrifice more opportunity than the conflict needs; the Warden may choose B. No universal rule.

**C — protection decides.** Two manifestations cost about the same to interrupt. One has protection and configuration better suited to the threat. The Warden chooses on the odds of bringing the body through intact — differentiation without any species hierarchy.

**D — spatial consequence.** The threat is far from A's best rooting environment. The Warden embodies A and travels to it. Afterwards A stands at the threat site, and the Warden must root it there or stay embodied and travel. Either has biological consequences. **Combat and World geography are one system.**

**E — senescent body.** A has closed its phase through Dormancy Commitment, and its manifestation still exists and is inhabitable. Using it interrupts no productive work for that phase; its body may nevertheless be declining, and core destruction risk remains. **Cheap opportunity cost is not cheap total risk.**

## 8. Deferred concrete combat design

AMO-Q027 keeps how distinct fighters are produced and everything concrete about differentiation: movement model, attack and defence structure, ability systems, matchup design, skill acquisition, technique ownership, and the exact influence of body morphology. AMO-Q109 keeps protection mechanics, hit determination and how condition changes a playable Amorpho; AMO-Q026 combat-state semantics and resources; AMO-Q025 cultivation's influence on combat; AMO-Q023 controls; AMO-Q059 and AMO-Q060 VR expression and parity; AMO-Q028 conflict contexts and consequences; AMO-Q130 Human combat. No new question was required.

**Result:** biology tells the Warden what a body is expensive to interrupt and risk; combat has to tell them which body is useful for this conflict; the decision lives in the tension between the two. And skill is not only the ability to hurt an opponent — it is the ability to bring the living body the Warden fights through out the other side.
